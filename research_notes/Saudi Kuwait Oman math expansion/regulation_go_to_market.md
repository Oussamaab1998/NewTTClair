# Regulation and go-to-market requirements: AI math tutor app for secondary students (ages ~15-18) in Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and Oman

Research as of 17 November 2026. This is research, not legal advice. Most findings come from law-firm briefings and official pages; several points conflict between sources, and each conflict is noted. Items marked "lawyer" need a local counsel opinion before launch.

Product assumptions used for this note: consumer iOS app sold by a Tunisian company, stores skill progress and interaction events per student, sends questions to a cloud LLM (EU/US) through the company's own backend, with no personal details in prompts.

---

## 1. Data protection: Saudi PDPL, Oman PDPL, Kuwait CITRA regulation (minors, registration, transfers, DPO, breach, penalties)

### Takeaway
Saudi Arabia and Oman have full GDPR-style laws that already apply and are enforced (Saudi since 14 Sept 2024, Oman since 5 Feb 2026); both require guardian consent for minors' data, and both restrict transfers abroad (Saudi: adequacy or SCCs plus risk assessment; Oman: the student's explicit consent plus an equivalence check). Kuwait has no general data protection law, only a CITRA regulation (Decision 26/2024) whose reach to foreign apps is disputed; it requires guardian consent for children, Arabic+English consent terms, and a very short breach notice (24 h per DLA Piper). Saudi rules may change soon: SDAIA opened a consultation on 6 Oct 2026 that would make processing of minors' data a DPIA trigger and require registration for controllers processing data of people lacking legal capacity.

### Cited Findings

**Saudi Arabia: Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL)**
- Law: Royal Decree No. M/19 (16 Sept 2021), amended by Royal Decree No. M/148 (27 Mar 2023); in force 14 Sept 2023; controllers had until 14 Sept 2024 to comply. The Implementing Regulations and the Transfer Regulations are in force; as of Feb 2026 SDAIA is actively enforcing. Regulator: Saudi Data & AI Authority (SDAIA). — [DLA Piper, Data Protection Laws of the World: Saudi Arabia](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=SA)
- Extraterritorial scope: the PDPL covers processing of personal data of individuals residing in KSA by entities outside KSA (so a Tunisian company serving Saudi students is in scope). — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=SA)
- Registration: under SDAIA rules certain controllers must register on the National Data Governance Platform; "in practice, most controllers must register." — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=SA)
- DPO required for: public entities processing at large scale; controllers whose primary activities involve regular and continuous large-scale monitoring of individuals; controllers whose core activities involve processing sensitive data. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=SA)
- Minors: Implementing Regulations Art. 13 — where the data subject lacks full or partial legal capacity, consent must come from the legal guardian; the controller must take appropriate steps to verify guardianship; Art. 13(3): guardian consent must not harm the child's interests, and the child must be able to exercise PDPL rights on reaching legal capacity. Art. 4: controllers handling children's data on a continuous or large scale must give privacy information in language suitable for the child. Art. 16(6) restricts disclosure that conflicts with the interests of a person lacking capacity. — [Bird & Bird, Children in the Digital World: Saudi Arabia](https://childreninthedigitalworld.twobirds.com/home/saudi-arabia)
- Age: the PDPL sets no age at which a child can consent alone; the Child Protection Law (Royal Decree No. M/14 of 1436H) treats anyone under 18 as a minor, and minors lack contractual capacity. So all users aged 15-17 need guardian consent. No specific age-verification or parental-control requirement exists; the National Framework for Children's Online Safety is guiding principles only. — [Bird & Bird](https://childreninthedigitalworld.twobirds.com/home/saudi-arabia)
- Source conflict on age: one secondary summary says only under-13s need parental consent; Bird & Bird ties capacity to the under-18 minor definition. — [search summary of Cookiebot/Usercentrics guides](https://cookiebot.com/en/saudi-arabia-personal-data-protection-law-pdpl) vs [Bird & Bird](https://childreninthedigitalworld.twobirds.com/home/saudi-arabia)
- Cross-border transfers: the amended Regulation on Personal Data Transfer outside the Kingdom was published 1 Sept 2024 and took effect on publication. Safeguards narrowed to three: SDAIA Standard Contractual Clauses (four versions: C2P, C2C, P2C, P2P; any modification invalidates them), Binding Corporate Rules, and certificates of accreditation. SDAIA is to publish an adequacy list. A risk assessment is required where transfers rely on appropriate safeguards, and for continuous or large-scale transfers of sensitive data. — [Mayer Brown, 9 Oct 2024](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2024/10/updates-to-saudi-arabias-personal-data-protection-regulations-sccs-guidelines-and-more); [Baker McKenzie](https://insightplus.bakermckenzie.com/bm/data-technology/saudi-arabia-updates-data-transfer-regulations-and-introduces-the-first-set-of-standard-contractual-clauses)
- Adequacy list: as of Feb 2025 "a list of adequate countries is not yet available"; no later confirmation found. — [Clyde & Co, Feb 2025](https://www.clydeco.com/zh/insights/2025/02/10-legal-updates-ksa-tech-and-data-laws)
- Transfers must not compromise national security or vital interests and must be limited to the minimum data needed; DLA Piper describes no general data-localization rule (sector rules aside). — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=SA)
- Breach: notify SDAIA via the National Data Governance Portal within 72 hours of becoming aware (where notification is required); notify affected individuals without undue delay where required. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=SA)
- Penalties: warning or fine up to SAR 5 million; doubled for repeat offenders; disclosing/publishing sensitive data with intent to harm or for benefit: up to 2 years' prison and/or SAR 3 million; confiscation, publication of decisions, and civil damages possible. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=SA)
- DRAFT (not in force): SDAIA published draft amendments to the Implementing Regulation on 6 Oct 2026, comments due 5 Nov 2026, effective 60 days after Gazette publication if adopted. Proposals: registration (Art. 34) required for controllers processing sensitive data or data of individuals lacking legal capacity, and for controllers transferring data outside the Kingdom (possibly covering non-KSA controllers); DPO rules and National Register rules repealed and folded into the Regulation, DPO contact submitted via SDAIA platform; DPIA required for processing "relating to minors or individuals with partial or no legal capacity" (no longer only large-scale), and for "emerging technologies" and solely automated decisions; information must be in "appropriate and simplified" language; new Art. 23(2) wording that controllers store data in the Kingdom while also allowing transfers (ambiguous whether a local copy is always required); breach-notice harm threshold removed; marketing messages always need consent; data-subject requests 30 days + 30 extension. — [Reed Smith, Oct 2026](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102o5at/sdaia-consults-on-extensive-amendments-to-the-saudi-pdpl-implementing-regulation/); [Al Tamimi, 9 Oct 2026](https://www.tamimi.com/news/sdaia-opens-public-consultation-on-proposed-amendments-to-the-pdpl-implementing-regulation/)

**Oman: Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL)**
- Law: Royal Decree No. 6/2022, published in Official Gazette issue 1429 on 13 Feb 2022, effective one year later. Executive Regulation: Ministerial Decision No. 34/2024 (Feb 2024). Transition extended by Ministerial Decision No. 6/2025 (Gazette 1580, 19 Jan 2025). Fully enforceable since 5 Feb 2026. Regulator: Ministry of Transport, Communications and IT (MTCIT). — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/omn/legal-updates/oman-personal-data-protection-law-entering-the-enforcement-phase); [NHIMG guide](https://nhimg.org/gulf-states-data-protection-laws-guide)
- Executive Regulation effective date is reported differently (29 Jan 2024, 4 Feb 2024 or 5 Feb 2024 depending on source). — [search summary of Al Tamimi/Lexis/Dentons](https://www.lexis.ae/2024/02/08/oman-data-protection-law-issued/)
- Children: the PDPL bars processing a child's data without the guardian's approval, except where processing serves the child's best interest; the Executive Regulation requires the guardian's express consent before processing, gives the guardian rights to access/update the child's data, forbids sharing the child's data without the guardian's explicit consent, and lets the controller ask the minor for the minimum guardian data needed to verify the guardian and get consent. — [Al Tamimi](https://www.tamimi.com/news/33608/); [Addleshaw Goddard, 12 Mar 2024](https://www.addleshawgoddard.com/en/insights/insights-briefings/2024/data-protection/executive-regulations-oman-personal-data-protection-law/); [decree.om](https://decree.om/?p=43958)
- Consent must be explicit and informed; privacy notices should be in Arabic (dual-language allowed); written data-subject requests answered within 45 days. — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/omn/legal-updates/oman-personal-data-protection-law-entering-the-enforcement-phase)
- Permits: processing Art. 5 sensitive data (genetic, biometric, health, ethnic origin, sex life, political/religious opinion, criminal records, security measures) requires an MTCIT permit; MTCIT has 45 days, silence = refusal, permit up to 5 years. (Children's data is not in this list.) — [NHIMG](https://nhimg.org/gulf-states-data-protection-laws-guide)
- DPO: mandatory (Art. 20); the regulator prefers the officer to be physically in Oman; contact details must be public. — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/omn/legal-updates/oman-personal-data-protection-law-entering-the-enforcement-phase); [NHIMG](https://nhimg.org/gulf-states-data-protection-laws-guide)
- Breach: notify MTCIT within 72 hours where rights may be harmed; notify data subjects within the same 72 hours where serious harm is likely; log every breach. — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/omn/legal-updates/oman-personal-data-protection-law-entering-the-enforcement-phase)
- Transfers abroad (Art. 23): require the data subject's explicit (written) consent, a check that the recipient's protection is at least equal to Omani law, a report kept ready for MTCIT, and no harm to national security; sensitive-data transfers may also need Cyber Defence Centre approval. Anonymised transfers are exempt. — [NHIMG](https://nhimg.org/gulf-states-data-protection-laws-guide); [CMS](https://cms.law/en/omn/legal-updates/oman-personal-data-protection-law-entering-the-enforcement-phase); [Securiti](https://securiti.ai/oman-personal-data-protection-law-pdpl/)
- Penalties: Art. 25 OMR 500-2,000; Art. 26 OMR 1,000-5,000; Art. 27 (security) OMR 5,000-10,000; Art. 28 OMR 15,000-20,000; Art. 29 (unlawful transfers) OMR 100,000-500,000; Art. 30 OMR 5,000-100,000 for legal persons; Ministry administrative fines up to OMR 2,000 per violation; permit suspension/cancellation. — [NHIMG](https://nhimg.org/gulf-states-data-protection-laws-guide); [CMS](https://cms.law/en/omn/legal-updates/oman-personal-data-protection-law-entering-the-enforcement-phase)
- Scope: the PDPL does not expressly claim extraterritorial reach, though non-Omani organisations may be indirectly affected. — [CMS](https://cms.law/en/omn/legal-updates/oman-personal-data-protection-law-entering-the-enforcement-phase)

**Kuwait: CITRA Data Privacy Protection Regulation**
- No general personal data protection statute and no national data protection authority. Main instrument: CITRA Data Privacy Protection Regulation, Administrative Decision No. 26 of 2024 (replacing the 2021 version); other relevant laws: Law No. 20 of 2014 (Electronic Transactions/E-Commerce) and Cybercrime Law No. 63 of 2015. — [DLA Piper: Kuwait](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=KW)
- Dates conflict: published in the Official Gazette 18 Feb 2024 with one year to comply ([NHIMG](https://nhimg.org/gulf-states-data-protection-laws-guide)) vs "enacted 26 Mar 2024, enforced 1 Jan 2025" ([Clym](https://clym.io/regulations/kuwait-data-privacy-protection-regulation-26-2024)).
- Scope conflict: DLA Piper says "service provider" is defined broadly enough to reach anyone operating a website, smart application or cloud service that collects personal data ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=KW)); Chambers 2026 says it applies only to CITRA licensees ([as reported by NHIMG](https://nhimg.org/gulf-states-data-protection-laws-guide)). Whether it covers a foreign app with no Kuwaiti licence is not stated.
- Consent: before providing a service, explain services and terms in Arabic and English, state purpose and method of use, and obtain consent. No registration requirement. No explicit DPO appointment rule, but DPO contact details are required in breach reports. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=KW)
- Children: parental/guardian consent required for children's data; no age threshold found in the sources. — [Clym](https://clym.io/regulations/kuwait-data-privacy-protection-regulation-26-2024); [Signzy](https://www.signzy.com/regulation-glossary/data-privacy-protection-regulation-CITRA)
- Breach: notify CITRA and affected users within 24 hours of awareness (individual notice not needed if data was effectively protected, e.g. encrypted) per [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=KW); contradicted by [Clym](https://clym.io/regulations/kuwait-data-privacy-protection-regulation-26-2024) (72 hours).
- Transfers: providers must record and disclose foreign destination countries; CITRA's Data Classification Policy (which kept Tier-4 data in Kuwait) was repealed in Feb 2024; no general localization rule. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=KW); [NHIMG](https://nhimg.org/gulf-states-data-protection-laws-guide). PwC reports no regulator authorisation is needed for transfers — [PwC 2024](https://www.pwc.com/m1/en/publications/documents/2024/navigating-cross-border-data-transfers-key-regulations-in-the-middle-east.pdf)
- Penalties: CITRA framework 1-5 years' imprisonment and/or KWD 500-20,000; E-Commerce Law: up to 3 years and fines of at least KWD 5,000 for disclosing personal information without consent; Cybercrime Law fines KWD 500-20,000 depending on offence. — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/index.html?t=law&c=KW)
- No draft general data protection law was found in 2025-2026 reporting. — [search of K&S, Chambers 2025](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2025/kuwait)

### Inferences
- All three markets: treat every 15-17-year-old as a minor needing verified guardian consent. Build one consent flow (guardian identity minimum data, guardian dashboard to view/edit/delete) and reuse it. This matches Saudi IR Art. 13, Oman's Executive Regulation, and Kuwait's CITRA rule.
- Saudi: because the product processes minors' data and transfers data abroad, assume registration on SDAIA's National Data Governance Platform is needed now ("most controllers") and almost certainly after the 2026 amendments. An adaptive tutor that logs every interaction could be argued to be "regular and continuous monitoring" for DPO purposes; appointing a DPO is the safer choice. A DPIA for minors' data and for the AI component will likely become mandatory if the draft is adopted.
- Saudi transfers to an EU/US backend and LLM: with no adequacy list published, the practical route is SDAIA SCCs (unmodified) with each processor (cloud host, LLM provider) plus a documented transfer risk assessment. The draft Art. 23(2) "store in the Kingdom" language could require a Saudi-hosted copy; watch the final text (lawyer).
- Oman: cross-border transfer needs the student's (guardian's) explicit consent, and the transfer fine bracket (OMR 100,000-500,000) is by far the largest in Oman's law. Put an explicit, separate transfer consent in the Omani onboarding, and keep the equivalence report on file. Consider hosting in-region if consent rates are low.
- Kuwait: the lightest regime on paper, but the 24-hour breach clock (if it applies) is the strictest of the three; incident response should be designed to meet 24 h.
- "No personal details in prompts" helps, but pseudonymous skill/event data linked to an account is still personal data under all three regimes.

### Gaps
- Official Arabic texts of the Saudi IR Art. 13 and the Oman Executive Regulation children's article were not retrieved; Oman's definition of "child" (age) was not found in the sources (Oman's Child Law likely sets 18, not verified here).
- One search result mentioned an Oman PDPL amendment by "Royal Decree 68/2026, effective 7 Sept 2026"; I could not verify it, and CMS/NHIMG report no 2026 amendment. Needs checking.
- Whether Kuwait's Decision 26/2024 reaches a foreign app with no Kuwaiti licence, and whether its breach window is 24 h or 72 h (lawyer).
- Whether Saudi requires a local representative for foreign controllers: not addressed by DLA Piper; not confirmed either way.
- Whether SDAIA has published the adequacy list after Feb 2025.

## 2. Edtech / e-learning licensing, Ministry of Education rules, tutoring and teacher restrictions

### Takeaway
Saudi Arabia is the only one of the three with a formal e-learning licensing regime (NELC), and its trigger is issuing completion certificates; a consumer exam-prep app that issues no certificates may fall outside it, but a separate 2004 Saudi law on "instructional materials" requires a Ministry licence for any curriculum-based material produced abroad and bans "providing direct answers to textbook questions or exercises" (fine up to SAR 200,000). Kuwait and Oman have no consumer edtech licence that I could find, but both restrict private tutoring by school teachers, and Oman's Ministry prohibits "unsupervised private systems" inside schools.

### Cited Findings

**Saudi Arabia**
- NELC eLearning Regulations approved by NELC Board decision No. 46/17/6 dated 11/5/1446H (about Nov 2024). Implementing Rules Art. 3-4: apply to government, private and non-profit entities, local or international, that provide eLearning. Art. 7: entities providing education or training through any eLearning mode in Saudi Arabia that "grant certificates of completion" must be licensed before starting. Art. 8 licence types: programs/curricula or training courses, in blended or distance mode. Art. 9 conditions: licences from relevant ministries, meet NELC standards, pay fees, technical integration and data sharing with the National eLearning Platform. Art. 10: decision within 5 working days. Art. 14: individuals may sell eLearning only through a licensed entity. Art. 15-16 sanctions: warning with 30 working days to fix, suspension/cancellation, order to stop, ban on advertising, public naming. No fee amounts published on the page. — [NELC Implementing Rules of the eLearning Regulations](https://nelc.gov.sa/en/regulations-and-standards/elearning-executive-rules); [NELC eLearning Regulation](https://nelc.gov.sa/en/regulations-and-standards/elearning-regulation)
- From May 2026, private-sector training providers complete technical integration with NELC's FutureX platform and then obtain program licences from TVTC (announced 25 Mar 2026); the notice speaks of "training providers," not school-level content. — [NELC news, 25 Mar 2026](https://nelc.gov.sa/en/media-center/news/unified-e-learning-licensing-private-sector-en)
- Law of the Production and Marketing of Instructional Materials, Royal Decree No. M/56 (12 Dec 2004): "Instructional Material: Any format that presents the contents of the curriculum, or part thereof"; Art. 2: applies to material "produced locally or abroad"; Art. 3: producing or marketing material that violates Sharia/public policy, violates the national Education Policy, or provides "direct answers to textbook questions or exercises" is prohibited; Art. 4: producers need a qualification certificate from the relevant agency; Art. 5: licence required before marketing, decision within 60 days (silence = approval), exam fee SAR 200/hour, minimum SAR 1,000, and final clearance by the media ministry under the printed-materials law; Art. 9: deposit a copy; Art. 12: fine up to SAR 200,000 and suspension of the material. — [Bureau of Experts official translation, hosted by MISA](https://misa.gov.sa/app/uploads/2025/08/Law-of-the-Production-and-Marketing-of-Instructional-Materials.pdf)
- New General Education Law: approved by the Council of Ministers 7 Jul 2026, published 24 Jul 2026, in force 20 Jan 2027; a new General Education Affairs Council (chaired by the Minister) approves rules on fees, co-education, non-Arabic teaching, online schools and private schools; rules replacing the private/foreign school regulations due by 20 Jan 2028; in-person teaching is the default and virtual-school rules are pending. — [Al Tamimi, 30 Sept 2026](https://www.tamimi.com/news/opening-a-school-in-saudi-arabia-what-the-new-education-law-means-for-international-educators/); [Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2652172)
- The new law also expands distance learning, electronic platforms and lifelong learning, and the Ministry will set rules for synchronous/asynchronous online learning. — [Zawya/Saudi Gazette](https://www.zawya.com/en/world/saudi-arabias-new-education-law-explained-key-changes-for-schools-teachers-and-students-409385); [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/gulf/saudi/saudi-arabia-approves-landmark-education-law-in-major-school-system-overhaul-1.500619923)
- Tutoring: a Ministry of Education circular allowed private evening lessons at schools and homes with fee caps (home lessons SR80 per class elementary, SR100 middle/high school); article date not shown. — [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/gulf/saudi/saudi-arabia-gives-green-light-for-private-evening-lessons-in-schools-and-homes-1.95798563). A Riyadh education directorate circular bars teachers from home-tutoring the same students they teach in the morning. — [Siasat](https://www.siasat.com/saudi-allows-teachers-to-provide-private-evening-lessons-in-schools-home-2591880)

**Kuwait**
- Ministry circulars of 1962 and 1964 barred teachers from private tutoring without explicit Ministry permission; a 2014 circular banned all private tutoring; in 2018 authorities shifted to a less confrontational approach. — [Bray & Hajar, ECNU Review of Education, 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/20965311221113350)
- The Ministry warned that expatriate teachers giving private tuition without approval face legal measures, including contract termination (older reports). — [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/gulf/kuwait/kuwait-to-sack-expat-teachers-giving-private-tuition-1.1966949); [Arab Times](https://www.arabtimesonline.com/news/expat-teachers-warned-private-tuition/)
- A market report claims private tutors need a Ministry licence; not confirmed by any official source. — [6Wresearch](https://www.6wresearch.com/industry-report/kuwait-private-tutoring-market)

**Oman**
- The Ministry of Education treats unauthorised private tutoring as illegal; a 2018 circular told principals to stop K-12 teachers from giving private tuition; "any commercial activity in education without validation from the competent authorities is illegal" (official quoted). — [Oman Observer, 2018](https://www.omanobserver.om/ampArticle/49198)
- Paid tutoring moved online during COVID-19 in breach of ministry bans on private commercial academic classes. — [Oman Observer](https://www.omanobserver.om/article/6082/Main/private-tuitions-flourish-during-pandemic)
- Ministry e-learning roadmap (27 Oct 2025): schools must integrate with the official Noor Education platform, and the Ministry prohibits "unsupervised private systems" to prevent content fragmentation. — [Oman Observer, 27 Oct 2025](https://www.omanobserver.om/article/1178733/oman/omans-schools-go-digital)
- Ministry of Labour: all private training activities, regardless of type or delivery method, placed under its direct supervision (Directorate General of Private Training Institutions); new private-training-institution licences were reported as paused. — [Oman Observer](https://www.omanobserver.om/article/1176921/oman/labour/labour-ministry-tightens-rules-on-private-training-in-oman); [Times of Oman](https://timesofoman.com/article/129577-no-new-licenses-for-the-establishment-of-private-training-institutions-in-oman-for-this-duration)

### Inferences
- Saudi NELC: if the app issues no completion certificates, the Art. 7 licence trigger is likely not met. Do not add "certificates" or "attestations" to the Saudi product without first getting a NELC/TVTC licence. A written confirmation from NELC (or a lawyer's opinion) is cheap insurance.
- Saudi Instructional Materials Law is the bigger risk for a curriculum-aligned math tutor: an app presenting Saudi curriculum content is plausibly "instructional material produced abroad". The "no direct answers to textbook exercises" ban fits the product's own hint-ladder rule (no full answer before the ladder), but worked solutions to official textbook exercises should be avoided in Saudi content. Whether the Ministry applies this 2004 law to apps in practice is unknown (lawyer + direct question to MoE).
- Do not recruit or advertise serving public-school teachers as tutors/content authors in any of the three countries without checking their employer's rules (Kuwait and Oman bans; Riyadh own-students rule). Use non-teaching subject experts or get written ministry permission.
- Oman's Noor-only rule targets schools; a consumer app sold to families is a different channel, but any B2G or school deal in Oman would have to go through Noor integration.

### Gaps
- No official Saudi MoE rule found that specifically licenses online tutoring apps; NELC fee amounts and licence term not published on the page.
- Whether the Saudi Instructional Materials Law implementing regulations cover digital apps and who the "relevant agency" is today for general-education content (likely MoE).
- No Kuwaiti or Omani statute or licence for consumer e-learning apps was found; Kuwait Private Education Department and Oman Ministry of Education/Higher Education should be asked directly.
- Current (2025-2026) Kuwaiti rules on teachers advertising tutoring online: not found.

## 3. Using national exam content: textbooks, past papers, Tahsili/Qudurat questions

### Takeaway
Oman's Ministry of Education expressly owns copyright in its curriculum books and forbids copying or storing them electronically without written permission (School Education Law, Royal Decree 31/2023, Art. 77). Saudi copyright law (2003) applies and ETEC treats leaking or photographing Qiyas test questions as a legal violation, so real Tahsili/Qudurat items must not be used; write original exam-style items instead. Kuwait's 2019 copyright law excludes official legislative/administrative texts, but whether past exam papers count is unclear.

### Cited Findings
- Saudi Copyright Law: Royal Decree No. M/41 of 2 Rajab 1424 (30 Aug 2003); now administered by the Saudi Authority for Intellectual Property (SAIP). — [WIPO Lex](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/legislation/details/3593); [MISA-hosted text](https://misa.gov.sa/app/uploads/2025/08/Copyright-Law.pdf)
- Saudi Instructional Materials Law Art. 3(3) bans "providing direct answers to textbook questions or exercises" in any instructional material, including material produced abroad. — [BOE translation via MISA](https://misa.gov.sa/app/uploads/2025/08/Law-of-the-Production-and-Marketing-of-Instructional-Materials.pdf)
- Qiyas (ETEC) publicly warned that "leaking or photographing" Qiyas test questions is a legal violation (2018 post). — [Qiyas on X](https://x.com/EtecQiyas/status/998201721355726848?lang=en)
- ETEC exam rules prohibit disclosure of protected test questions or content (third-party guide); prep sites present their items as independent exam-style questions, not official or recalled items. — [open-exam-prep](https://open-exam-prep.com/exams/academic/sa-etec-assessment)
- Oman School Education Law, Royal Decree 31/2023, Art. 77: the Ministry of Education owns copyright in the books and curricula it prepares; they may not be printed, copied, reproduced, translated, or stored on any electronic system without prior written permission; the Ministry has no formal licensing mechanism for such permissions (author's observation). — [decree.om blog, 18 Jun 2023](https://blog.decree.om/?p=829)
- Kuwait Copyright and Related Rights Law No. 75 of 2019, published in the Official Gazette 28 Jul 2019, replacing the 1999 law. — [WIPO Lex](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/legislation/details/19908); [official Arabic text, NCCAL](https://nccal.gov.kw/Content/Images/uploaded/قانون حق المؤلف/حقوق المؤلف75.pdf)
- Kuwait Law 75/2019 Art. 4 reportedly excludes official legislative, administrative and judicial texts (and their translations) from protection (secondary wiki summary). — [Wikimedia Commons: Kuwait](https://c.enwp.org/wiki/Commons:CRT/Kuwait)

### Inferences
- Safe default for all three countries: write original exercises "in the style of" national exams; cite exam year and topic only as metadata; do not reproduce Ministry textbook pages, figures or past papers verbatim. This matches the project's existing rule of not copying official corrections.
- Saudi Tahsili/Qudurat: never use recalled or leaked real items; ETEC treats this as a legal violation.
- Oman: using Ministry textbook content in the app needs written Ministry permission (Art. 77); expect it to be slow.
- Kuwait: past exam papers might fall under the "official administrative texts" exclusion, which would make them free to reuse; this is uncertain (lawyer).

### Gaps
- Saudi: no provision found on who owns MoE textbooks or Ministry past papers, or whether government works are excluded; ETEC's penalty provisions not found.

## 4. AI rules: SDAIA and Saudi guidelines; Kuwait and Oman AI policy

### Takeaway
None of the three countries has a binding AI statute as of Nov 2026. Saudi Arabia has non-binding but influential SDAIA AI Ethics Principles (2023) and Generative AI Guidelines (2024); the binding AI-related rules come through the PDPL, and the draft 2026 PDPL amendments would require DPIAs for "emerging technologies" and solely automated decisions. Kuwait (CAIT strategy 2025-2028, CITRA AI-adoption guidelines consultation in 2026) and Oman (public policy on safe and ethical AI) have strategies and policies, not obligations specific to education apps or minors.

### Cited Findings
- Saudi: no general AI statute; SDAIA governs AI through the PDPL plus soft-law documents: AI Ethics Principles (2023) and Generative AI Guidelines (separate documents for government and for the public, dated Jan 2024 by one source, May 2025 for the public version by another), covering content authenticity, watermarking, vendor due diligence, data classification, human oversight and harm prevention; these are non-binding but referenced by procurement and sector regulators. — [Nemko](https://digital.nemko.com/regulations/ai-regulation-in-saudi-arabia); [vision2030.ai](https://vision2030.ai/regulation/saudi-arabia-ai-regulation-sdaia/)
- Saudi: the draft Global AI Hub Law was published by the Communications, Space & Technology Commission (CST) for consultation 14 Apr-14 May 2025; it covers hosting foreign AI infrastructure and data, not AI use; no confirmation it was enacted. — [GRC Lens (secondary)](https://www.grclens.net/blog/ai-governance-saudi-uae-pakistan-2026)
- Saudi: draft PDPL Implementing Regulation amendments (Oct 2026) would require a DPIA for "emerging technologies" (undefined), solely automated decisions affecting data subjects' rights or interests, and processing relating to minors. — [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/viewpoints/102o5at/sdaia-consults-on-extensive-amendments-to-the-saudi-pdpl-implementing-regulation/)
- Kuwait: National AI Strategy 2025-2028 launched in draft by the Central Agency for Information Technology (CAIT) in early 2025; priority sectors include education; it is non-binding; a 2026 ILO paper says it is still being finalised. — [Nemko: Kuwait](https://digital.nemko.com/regulations/kuwait-ai-regulation); [ILO 2026](https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/2026-03/Adoption%20of%20AI%20and%20productivity%20growth%20in%20Kuwait's%20private%20sector.pdf)
- Kuwait: CITRA consulted on "Guidelines for Adopting AI in the State of Kuwait," responses until 20 Apr 2026. — [AI Policy Tracker: Kuwait](https://aipolicytracker.org/jurisdictions/kuwait)
- Oman: Public Policy for the Safe and Ethical Use of Artificial Intelligence Systems; National Program for AI and Advanced Digital Technologies (under Oman Vision 2040), which suggested updating copyright law for generative AI; a draft National AI Policy is also reported. — [Library of Congress FALQs, Dec 2024](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2024/12/falqs-ai-regulations-in-the-gulf-cooperation-council-member-states-part-two/); [JAIR 2025 comparative study](https://jair.org/index.php/jair/article/download/17619/27175/45490)

### Inferences
- The project's existing design (correctness computed by the engine, not the LLM; no personal data in prompts; human-reviewed content; clear labelling that the tutor is AI) already lines up with the SDAIA principles on human oversight, transparency and harm prevention. Document it in a short "AI use" note per market; it will be useful in Saudi B2G/school procurement where SDAIA principles are referenced.
- If the Saudi PDPL amendments pass, the AI planner choosing the next step for a minor would likely need a DPIA (minors + emerging tech); prepare one now.
- No rule found that bars AI tutoring for minors in any of the three countries.

### Gaps
- Exact title/date of SDAIA's public Generative AI Guidelines (Jan 2024 vs May 2025) and any SDAIA guidance specific to education or children.
- Oman MTCIT AI guidance texts and dates were not retrieved from official sites.
- Saudi Ministry of Education or NELC rules on AI in learning content: none found.

## 5. Doing business: selling without a local entity, investment licences, VAT

### Takeaway
App-store sales can start without a local company: Apple collects and remits VAT on App Store sales in Saudi Arabia (since 2018) and adjusts developer proceeds for Oman's 5% VAT, and Kuwait has no VAT. A local entity becomes necessary for direct local payments, B2B/B2G contracts, and any Saudi licence that requires a Saudi Commercial Registration. In Saudi Arabia the old "MISA licence" has been replaced (2025) by investment registration, typically 3-10 business days plus about a week for the Commercial Registration.

### Cited Findings
- Apple began collecting taxes on App Store sales to customers in Saudi Arabia on 1 Jan 2018 and administers collection and remittance to the tax authority. — [MacRumors, 25 Jan 2018](https://macrumors.com/2018/01/25/apple-tax-pricing-changes-foreign-app-stores)
- Saudi VAT rose from 5% to 15% (Apple adjusted developer proceeds in 2020). — [MacRumors, 1 Sept 2020](https://www.macrumors.com/2020/09/01/apple-raising-app-store-prices-tax-changes)
- Oman: Apple notice "New value-added tax of 5%"; App Store prices unchanged, developer proceeds adjusted. — [App Developer Magazine, Jan 2022](https://appdevelopermagazine.com/app-price-and-tax-changes-from-apple/)
- Saudi VAT electronic-marketplace "deemed supplier" rules (VAT Implementing Regulations Art. 47): amendments effective from April 2025, expanded scope from 1 Jan 2026; where a platform facilitates electronic services to Saudi customers by non-resident suppliers, the platform is the deemed supplier and VAT liability shifts away from the underlying supplier; non-resident platforms must register with no value threshold. — [Grant Thornton Saudi Arabia](https://www.grantthornton.sa/en/insights/articles-and-publications/vat_and_electronic_marketplace_in_saudi_arabia/)
- Oman VAT 5%; non-resident suppliers of digital services to consumers have no registration threshold, register via the Oman Tax Authority portal and need an Oman-resident responsible person/tax representative; B2B-only supplies are reverse-charged. — [Kintsugi](https://trykintsugi.com/sales-tax-guides/middle-east/oman); [Anrok](https://www.anrok.com/vat-software-digital-services/oman); [OTA VAT Taxpayer Guide (Electronic Commerce)](https://tms.taxoman.gov.om/portal/documents/20126/1414820/VAT+Taxpayer+Guide+(Electronic+Commerce).pdf/70d52b04-4177-2fe8-2d1c-836d6d831466?t=1733169614944)
- Kuwait: no VAT law in force, no registration and no VAT charged as of 31 Jul 2026; the government's four-year plan reportedly rules out VAT before 2028. — [TaxInMe (secondary)](https://www.taxinme.com/articles/kuwait-vat-status-2026.php); [GCC VAT 2026 overview](https://taxnews.ae/gcc-countries-and-vat-in-2026/)
- Saudi Investment Law in force since the start of 2025: the "MISA licence" no longer exists in its old form; foreign investors register their investment with MISA. Registration usually 3-10 business days; Commercial Registration about a week later; home-country document attestation typically 2-4 weeks; fee set by MISA on approval, payable within 15 business days. Old regime: SAR 2,000 plus Investor Relations subscription SAR 10,000 (year 1) and SAR 60,000/year after, reported suspended in 2026. — [Alliant (consultancy, secondary)](https://alliant.sa/guides/misa-investment-registration-saudi-arabia)
- Saudi digital content platform licences require a Saudi Commercial Registration related to the service (valid 10 years). — [Al Tamimi](https://turtl.tamimi.com/story/streaming-now-compliance-with-the-saudi-digital-content-platform-regulations/)
- Kuwait: KDIPA can license up to 100% foreign ownership in any sector except a negative list; approvals are described as rare (69 fully foreign-owned companies at last report). Education is listed among sectors open to 100% foreign investment by one overview. — [KDIPA Investment Guide 2016](https://kdipa.gov.kw/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/KDIPA-Investment-Guide-2016.pdf); [GoGlobal](https://goglobal.com/blog/entity-establishment/middle-east-100-foreign-ownership-whats-real-whats-conditional-whats-nearly-impossible/); [Marmore](https://www.marmoremena.com/en/insights/foreign-ownership-laws-in-the-gcc/)
- Oman: Foreign Capital Investment Law, Royal Decree 50/2019, allows 100% foreign ownership in most commercial activities, subject to a prohibited-activities list; education-sector rules may sit under a separate statute. — [BSA Law](https://bsalaw.com/insight/the-new-foreign-capital-investment-law-in-oman/); [Al Tamimi: prohibited list](https://www.tamimi.com/news/oman-foreign-investment-definitive-list-of-prohibited-activities-issued); [Al Tamimi education update 2022](https://turtl.tamimi.com/story/law-update-education-in-mena-2022/page/9)

### Inferences
- Phase 1 (lowest cost): sell subscriptions only through App Store / Google Play from the Tunisian company. Apple acts as the collector of VAT in Saudi Arabia and adjusts proceeds in Oman, so the developer most likely has no VAT filing of its own for in-app sales there (confirm with a tax adviser). Kuwait: no VAT.
- Direct web/payment-link sales to consumers would make the Tunisian company itself a non-resident supplier of electronic services: Saudi 15% and Oman 5% registration from the first sale, plus an Omani tax representative.
- A Saudi entity (MISA registration + CR) becomes necessary for: Saudi B2B/B2G contracts, local payment gateways, any NELC/TVTC or CST licence that requires a Saudi CR, and Saudi-hosted data if the PDPL amendment requires it. Budget weeks to a few months and local counsel; exact current MISA fees could not be confirmed from an official source.
- Kuwait and Oman entities are only needed for B2B/B2G or local payments; Kuwait's KDIPA route is slow and selective.

## 6. Content and cultural rules; app-store age ratings

### Takeaway
For Saudi Arabia, content must not violate Sharia, public policy or the national Education Policy (Instructional Materials Law Art. 3), and the Child Protection Law bars commercial exploitation of children through marketing. Influencer marketing in Saudi Arabia requires a Mawthooq licence for the creator. On Apple's App Store, use the new 13+ rating tier (since 2025) or set a higher minimum age; no country-specific age-rating law for apps was found.

### Cited Findings
- Saudi Instructional Materials Law Art. 3: prohibits instructional material violating Sharia, public policy and laws, or the approved Education Policy; Art. 5(d): a licence is final only after clearance by the media ministry under the Law of Printed Materials and Publication. — [BOE translation via MISA](https://misa.gov.sa/app/uploads/2025/08/Law-of-the-Production-and-Marketing-of-Instructional-Materials.pdf)
- Saudi Child Protection Law Art. 3.14 requires authorities to prevent exploitation of children through commercial marketing; Executive Regulations Art. 3(d) require protection from harmful media (obscene, criminal or age-unsuitable). No specific rules on targeted advertising or direct marketing to children; PDPL consent rules apply to direct marketing. — [Bird & Bird](https://childreninthedigitalworld.twobirds.com/home/saudi-arabia)
- Saudi Mawthooq licence: content creators earning advertising revenue on social media in the Kingdom must hold a GCAM permit (covers X, Instagram, YouTube, TikTok, Snapchat, Facebook). — [Arab News](https://arab.news/2xun3); [Saudipedia](https://saudipedia.com/en/what-is-the-mawthooq-license)
- Saudi CST Digital Content Platforms Regulations set licence/registration/notification tiers for video OTT, audio-on-demand and gaming platforms. — [Chambers TMT 2026: Saudi Arabia](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/tmt-2026/saudi-arabia/trends-and-developments)
- Saudi new General Education Law: gender separation after early childhood does not automatically apply to private institutions; controls still to be written. — [ARY News](https://arynews.tv/saudi-arabia-new-education-law-explained); [Al Tamimi](https://www.tamimi.com/news/opening-a-school-in-saudi-arabia-what-the-new-education-law-means-for-international-educators/)
- Apple App Store: new tiers 4+, 9+, 13+, 16+, 18+ (12+ and 17+ removed); new questionnaire questions (in-app controls, capabilities, medical/wellness, violence) had to be answered by 31 Jan 2026 or app updates are blocked; developers can set a higher minimum age. — [9to5Mac, 24 Jul 2025](https://9to5mac.com/2025/07/24/apple-notifies-developers-of-new-app-store-age-rating-system/); [MacRumors, 25 Jul 2025](https://www.macrumors.com/2025/07/25/apple-overhauls-app-store-age-ratings/)
- Google Play uses IARC ratings issued per region; content rating is separate from the "target audience" setting. — [Google Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/188189?hl=en-GB)

### Inferences
- A math tutor has low content risk. Practical rules for illustrations and examples: modest dress in any human images, no religious references or jokes, no alcohol/gambling/dating contexts in word problems (avoid "betting" framings in probability exercises; use dice, cards only with care, or neutral objects), use local names and currencies, avoid maps with disputed borders.
- Probability content commonly uses cards and games of chance; in Saudi Arabia these could be read against Sharia/public-policy content rules. Prefer urns, balls, dice and draws (lawyer/teacher review for Saudi edition).
- App Store: set 13+ (or 4+/9+ with a declared minimum age) and use the store's teen settings; a minors-focused app with an AI chat should disclose AI use and parental controls in the store listing.
- Any paid influencer campaign in Saudi Arabia: only use creators who hold a Mawthooq licence; avoid ads that pressure children (Child Protection Law Art. 3.14).

### Gaps
- Kuwait Ministry of Information and Oman Ministry of Information content rules for apps/educational content: not researched in depth (no sources found).
- GCAM/GAMR rules specific to educational apps: none found.
- Whether the CST Digital Content Platforms Regulations were finalised and whether any category covers education apps.

### Practical steps (synthesis across sections; not legal advice)
1. Launch order: Saudi Arabia and Oman have enforced data laws; Kuwait is lighter on data but has the strictest breach window. Start with App Store sales only (no local entity, no direct payments).
2. Build one guardian-consent flow (verified guardian, minimum data, guardian view/edit/delete, Arabic notice, simplified language for the teen) for all three countries.
3. Saudi: register on SDAIA's National Data Governance Platform; appoint a DPO; sign SDAIA SCCs (unmodified) with cloud and LLM processors; write a transfer risk assessment and a DPIA covering minors and the AI planner; 72-hour breach runbook. Re-check after SDAIA adopts the Oct 2026 amendments (possible Saudi-hosted copy requirement).
4. Oman: appoint a DPO (ideally Oman-based); add explicit consent for transfers abroad; keep the equivalence report; Arabic privacy notice; 72-hour breach runbook.
5. Kuwait: Arabic+English terms and consent; record destination countries of transfers; 24-hour breach runbook.
6. Saudi content: no certificates (stays outside NELC licensing); no direct answers to official textbook exercises; original exercises only (no real Qiyas/Tahsili items); ask a Saudi lawyer whether the Instructional Materials Law licence (SAR 1,000+ exam fee, 60 days) applies to the app.
7. Oman content: do not reproduce Ministry textbooks without written permission (School Education Law Art. 77).
8. Do not use serving public-school teachers as paid tutors or ad faces without employer permission.
9. Budget for local counsel in each country (data protection opinion + content/licensing opinion) before launch; a Saudi entity only when B2B/B2G or direct payments start.
